Abstract

The local coordination of metal–nitrogen–carbon catalysts governs the binding of oxygen-containing intermediates, which is important for oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) activity. It is necessary to clarify the coordination environment that metal sites adopt under realistic reaction conditions, and how this coordination affects the binding and conversion of ORR intermediates. Here, we prepare an FeCoMn–NC catalyst, in which FeCo alloy particles coexist with dispersed Mn species. The catalyst achieves a half-wave potential of 0.885 V versus the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) in 0.1 M KOH and retains 96.5% of its initial current after 238 h. Surface coverage calculations show that the FeCo particle surface is covered by O* and the Mn site carries an axial OH ligand at 0.78 V (vs. RHE) and pH 13. The calculated limiting potential of FeCoMn–NC is 0.78 V, which is much higher than 0.34 V of FeCo–NC and 0.26 V of Mn–NC. Compared with the oxo-ligated Mn site in Mn–NC, FeCoMn–NC forms a stronger Mn–O bond with OOH*, which favors stabilization of the first intermediate. Compared with the FeCo–NC, the FeCoMn–NC shows the weaker binding with OH*, which favors OH* removal in the final step. The axial-hydroxyl-ligated Mn site accommodates both OOH* formation and OH* removal and attains moderate intermediate binding. This work comprehensively investigates the effects of surface coverage, axial coordination and intermediate binding under reaction conditions, and provides a basis for understanding the ORR behavior of catalysts in which particles and dispersed metal sites coexist.

KEYWORDS: oxygen reduction reaction, M–N–C catalyst, FeCo alloy, dispersed Mn site, axial ligand, surface coverage, operando infrared spectroscopy, density functional theory

INTRODUCTION

Metal–nitrogen–carbon (M–N–C) catalysts are promising alternatives to platinum for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) in fuel cells and metal–air batteries.^(1,2) Their dispersed metal centers provide coordination environments that can be tailored to control the binding of oxygen-containing intermediates.^(3,4) Effective ORR catalysis requires these sites to stabilize intermediates sufficiently for their formation while allowing subsequent conversion and removal. Excessively strong binding can impede the removal of oxygenated species, whereas insufficient stabilization can make intermediate formation unfavorable.^(5,6) Improving M–N–C catalysts therefore requires understanding how the local environment of the metal center governs these competing requirements.

To tune intermediate binding, researchers have modified the coordinating atoms, carbon framework, and neighboring metal species around dispersed metal centers. Among these approaches, combining metal particles or clusters with M–N_(x) sites has enabled the development of active composite catalysts. Studies of Fe–N–C catalysts have shown that Fe/Fe₃C nanoparticles and metal clusters can modify the electronic environment of dispersed sites and their interactions with oxygen-containing intermediates.^(7,8) Understanding the catalytic behavior of such composites also requires considering how their surfaces interact with adsorbates under operating conditions.^(9,10) Oxygen-containing species can occupy particle surfaces or coordinate to dispersed metal centers,^(11,12) changing the environment in which subsequent intermediates bind.^(13,14) How these adsorbates alter intermediate binding in catalysts containing both particles and dispersed sites remains to be clarified.

Here, we report an FeCoMn–NC catalyst containing FeCo alloy particles and dispersed Mn sites and examine how surface coverage under operating conditions affects the binding and conversion of ORR intermediates. Surface coverage calculations identify O* coverage on the FeCo particle surface and axial OH coordination at Mn at 0.78 V versus RHE and pH 13.^(15,16) The calculated limiting potential reaches 0.78 V. Compared with Mn–NC, FeCoMn–NC forms a stronger Mn–O bond to OOH*, whereas its OH* binding is weaker than at the multimetal site of FeCo–NC. These results reveal how operating-state axial hydroxyl ligation tunes intermediate binding at Mn sites to accommodate both OOH* formation and OH* removal, thereby improving ORR thermodynamics.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

FeCoMn–NC was prepared by pyrolysis of an Fe/Co/Mn-containing zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF) precursor (Figure 1a). The precursor was synthesized from the metal sources and 2-methylimidazole in methanol and then pyrolyzed at 1000 °C for 2 h under Ar/H₂. The product was not acid-leached, so that the metal particles exposed at the surface of the carbon framework were retained. FeCo–NC and Mn–NC were prepared by the same procedure without the Mn source and without the Fe and Co sources, respectively.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Figure 1b) shows that the carbon framework of FeCoMn–NC retains the rhombic dodecahedral shape of the ZIF precursor and is approximately 1 μm across. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images show metal particles, predominantly 20–100 nm in size, dispersed throughout the carbon matrix (Figure 1c and Figure S1). These particles are primarily body-centered cubic (bcc) FeCo. Their X-ray diffraction (XRD) reflections at 44.77°, 65.23°, and 82.69° correspond to the bcc FeCo (110), (200), and (211) planes, respectively (Figure 1d). The reflection near 25.9° corresponds to the carbon (002) planes. Two weak peaks near 43.9° and 51.1°, indexed to the (111) and (200) planes of a face-centered cubic (fcc) structure, indicate a minor fcc phase. In the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image, a particle is surrounded by graphitic carbon layers with a lattice spacing of approximately 0.34 nm, which corresponds to the carbon (002) planes (Figure 1e and Figure S2). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping locates Fe and Co in the particles and Mn throughout the carbon matrix (Figure 1f). These results suggest that FeCoMn–NC consists of FeCo alloy particles dispersed in the carbon matrix and Mn species homogeneously distributed throughout the matrix.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) probes the surface composition and chemical states of FeCoMn–NC (Figure S3 and Table S1). The N 1s spectrum is deconvoluted into pyridinic, metal-coordinated, pyrrolic, graphitic, and oxidized N components, with graphitic N as the largest fraction (41.6%). The metal-coordinated N component at 399.3 eV supports the presence of metal–N sites. The Co 2p spectrum contains both metallic and oxidized components, whereas the Mn 2p spectrum contains only oxidized components. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) provides element-specific information on the local structures of Fe, Co, and Mn. The Fe and Co K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra resemble those of the corresponding metal foils more closely than those of the oxide references, indicating that Fe and Co are predominantly metallic (Figure 2a, b). In contrast, the Mn K-edge XANES spectrum resembles that of MnPc more closely than that of Mn foil, indicating that Mn is not metallic (Figure 2c).

The Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of Fe and Co exhibit strong peaks near 2.2 Å, assigned to metal–metal scattering (Figure 2d, e). The Mn spectrum instead shows a main peak near 1.3 Å without a resolved metal–metal scattering peak, consistent with predominantly light-element neighbors (Figure 2f). Fitting with a two-shell bcc structural model yields coordination-shell distances of 2.46 and 2.84 Å for Fe and 2.45 and 2.84 Å for Co. These values agree with the first- and second-shell distances of bcc FeCo (2.47 and 2.86 Å for a = 2.857 Å) and thus support the XRD assignment. First-shell fitting of the Mn spectrum yields an average Mn–N/O coordination number of 4.9 ± 0.6 and a bond distance of 1.99 ± 0.04 Å (Figures S4 and S5 and Table S2). The wavelet-transform intensity maxima of Fe and Co occur at relatively high k, further supporting metal–metal coordination (Figure 2g, h). The Mn intensity is instead concentrated at low k, consistent with light-element coordination (Figure 2i and Figure S6). Together, the XRD, EDS, XPS, and XAS results show that FeCoMn–NC contains bcc FeCo alloy particles and Mn single atoms.

The ORR activity was evaluated by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) on a rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) in O₂-saturated 0.1 M KOH. At 1600 rpm, FeCoMn–NC exhibits a half-wave potential of 0.885 V versus RHE (Figure 3a, b and Table S3). This value exceeds those of FeCo–NC and commercial Pt/C by 21 mV and that of Mn–NC by 45 mV. The Tafel slope of FeCoMn–NC (40 mV dec⁻¹) is also the lowest of the four catalysts, indicating faster apparent ORR kinetics (Figure 3c). At 0.90 V, the kinetic current density of FeCoMn–NC (2.89 mA cm⁻²) is much higher than those of the other three catalysts (Figure 3d). The kinetic current is corrected for mass transport and therefore reflects the intrinsic activity. FeCoMn–NC outperforms both FeCo–NC and Mn–NC in all three metrics. Its activity therefore cannot be attributed to either component alone.

Figure 3e and Table S4 compare the half-wave potential of FeCoMn–NC with those of reported M–N–C catalysts in 0.1 M KOH.^(8,17–19) FeCoMn–NC shows the highest value in this comparison, exceeding those of single-atom, single-metal, and bimetallic catalysts. The limiting current of all four catalysts increases with the rotation rate from 400 to 2500 rpm, as expected for diffusion-limited O₂ reduction (Figure S7). Cyclic voltammetry confirms that the reduction near 0.8 V originates from O₂ rather than from the catalyst itself. A cathodic peak appears at this potential in O₂-saturated electrolyte but is absent in N₂-saturated electrolyte (Figure S8). FeCoMn–NC also shows a larger capacitive current than FeCo–NC at the same scan rates, indicating a larger electrochemically accessible surface area. The RRDE measurements provide the electron-transfer number (n) and the H₂O₂ yield (Figure 3f). FeCoMn–NC shows an average n of 3.83 and an average H₂O₂ yield of 8.3% over 0.30–0.75 V, consistent with a predominantly four-electron pathway. This selectivity is slightly lower than that of Pt/C (n = 3.94, 3.1% H₂O₂), and the difference arises mainly at low potentials. The H₂O₂ yield of FeCoMn–NC decreases as the potential increases, and above about 0.75 V it falls below that of Pt/C. The measured ring currents give the same comparison (Figure S9). FeCoMn–NC retains 96.5% of its initial current after 238 h at 0.516 V and 1600 rpm (Figure 3g). To examine whether the half-wave potential shifts, LSV curves were recorded before and after a 31.6 h test. The half-wave potential shows no decrease (+0.8 mV, Figure 3h and Figure S10). Both results show that FeCoMn–NC is stable under prolonged operation.

Operando attenuated total reflection surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) was used to monitor the interfacial species on FeCoMn–NC in O₂-saturated 0.1 M KOH (Figure 4). Figure 4a shows the difference spectra between 3650 and 1000 cm⁻¹ at potentials from 1.2 to 0 V versus RHE, referenced to the spectrum at 1.2 V. The bands near 3307 and 1633 cm⁻¹ correspond to the O–H stretching and H–O–H bending modes of interfacial water, respectively, and the band near 1215 cm⁻¹ is assigned to the ORR intermediate OOH*.^(20,21) Figure 4b shows these three regions enlarged, with the spectra offset by potential. As the potential is lowered from 1.2 to 0 V, the negative O–H and H–O–H bands deepen, showing that the state of the interfacial water changes with potential. Meanwhile, the positive OOH* band grows, indicating that the ORR on FeCoMn–NC follows the associative pathway (Figure 4c). Overall, the FeCoMn–NC surface remains covered by interfacial species whose composition changes with potential.

The free energies of many different coverage configurations were therefore compared to determine the surface states of the three catalysts at various potentials and pH (Figure 5 and Table S5). As shown in Figure 5a–c, the surfaces of all three catalysts are covered by H* at negative potentials, and adsorbed O-containing species become more stable as the potential increases. As shown in Figure 5d–f, the surface coverage of FeCoMn–NC changes from 8H* to 8O* with increasing potential, then to 8O* + Mn–OH*, and finally to 8O* + Mn–O*. The surface coverage of FeCo–NC changes only from 8H* to 8O*, whereas that of Mn–NC changes from 3H* to the clean surface and then to 1O* and 2O*. Figures S13–S16 and S17–S22 give the corresponding free-energy data and structures, respectively. At 0.78 V versus RHE (a representative ORR potential)¹¹ and pH 13, which corresponds to the 0.1 M KOH electrolyte, the surface coverage is 8O* + Mn–OH* for FeCoMn–NC, 8O* for FeCo–NC, and 1O* for Mn–NC. The corresponding structures are shown in Figure 5g and Figure S24.

On these covered surfaces, obtained with the actual potential and solution pH taken into account, the free energies of the four-step associative pathway of O₂ → OOH* → O* → OH* → H₂O were then calculated. Here, ΔG₁, ΔG₂, ΔG₃, and ΔG₄ refer to the reaction free energies of OOH* formation, OOH* conversion to O*, O* conversion to OH*, and OH* removal, respectively (Table S7). On FeCoMn–NC and Mn–NC, the intermediates bind to Mn, and on FeCo–NC to the O-covered particle (Table S8 and Figure S25). Figure 5h compares the three pathways at 0.78 V, and Figure S26 shows them at 0 V. On FeCo–NC, O* binds too strongly, so its conversion to OH* is uphill by 0.44 eV. On Mn–NC, OOH* binds too weakly, so its formation is uphill by 0.52 eV. On FeCoMn–NC, the largest step is OOH* formation, which is uphill by less than 0.01 eV, and the other three steps are downhill. The limiting potentials of FeCoMn–NC, FeCo–NC, and Mn–NC are therefore 0.78, 0.34, and 0.26 V, corresponding to theoretical overpotentials of 0.45, 0.89, and 0.97 V (Table S7). The order of the limiting potentials agrees with that of the measured half-wave potentials (Figure 3b), and the moderate intermediate binding of FeCoMn–NC accounts for its higher activity than FeCo–NC and Mn–NC.

To understand the origin of this moderate binding, we analyzed the electronic structure of the reaction sites before and after the intermediates adsorb, together with the bonding and charge transfer between the sites and the intermediates (Figure 6). Before the reaction, the Mn atoms in FeCoMn–NC and Mn–NC have nearly the same occupied d-band center, and both are lower than that of the bonded metals in FeCo–NC (Figure 6a and Table S9). The d-band center therefore cannot explain why the two Mn atoms bind the intermediates differently. In contrast, the axial Mn–OH bond in FeCoMn–NC is much weaker than the axial Mn=O bond in Mn–NC, with negative integrated crystal orbital Hamilton population (−ICOHP) values of 2.64 and 8.03 eV (Figure 6b). The Mn atom in FeCoMn–NC also has a much larger local magnetic moment (Figure 6c). Its spin-up and spin-down 3d states are strongly unbalanced, whereas those of Mn in Mn–NC are nearly balanced, with almost all 3d electrons paired. The difference between the Mn atoms in FeCoMn–NC and Mn–NC therefore lies in the axial ligand adsorbed from the electrolyte before the reaction.

After the intermediates adsorb, the metal 3d states overlap with the O 2p states of the intermediates below the Fermi level in each catalyst, forming metal–O bonds (Figure 6d, Figures S27 and S28). The Mn–O bonds formed with OOH* and OH* are stronger in FeCoMn–NC than in Mn–NC, as shown by their larger −ICOHP values (Figure 6e and Table S9). Mn in FeCoMn–NC therefore holds OOH* more firmly, which makes OOH* formation easier than on Mn–NC, where this step limits the ORR. Upon adsorption, electrons move from the catalyst to the intermediates, and the amount transferred reflects how strongly they interact. Both the electrons lost by the bonded metals (Figure 6f) and those gained by the intermediates (Figure 6g and Figure S30) increase from Mn–NC to FeCoMn–NC to FeCo–NC. FeCoMn–NC thus binds the intermediates moderately. This moderate binding places FeCoMn–NC near the apex of the activity volcano (Figure 6h), where OOH* formation and OH* removal are relatively balanced. Because ΔG_(OOH*) − ΔG_(OH*) is nearly constant for the three catalysts (Table S7), the limiting potential can be approximated as a function of ΔG_(OH*) alone,^(5,22,23) which gives the volcano in Figure 6h.^(6,24) The construction of this volcano is described in Section 2.8 of the Supporting Information. Figure 6i summarizes the mechanism. At the ORR working potential and pH 13, O* and OH from the electrolyte adsorb on the FeCo particles and on the neighboring Mn, respectively, with the OH bound axially. The ORR proceeds on this Mn single atom.

CONCLUSIONS

We prepared an FeCoMn–NC catalyst in which FeCo alloy particles coexist with dispersed Mn species and accounted for its favorable ORR performance by combining surface coverage, reaction free-energy, and electronic-structure analyses. At the operating potential and pH, the coverage calculations give O* coverage on the FeCo particle surface and axial OH coordination at the Mn site, and the corresponding limiting potential reaches 0.78 V. Bonding and charge analyses show that FeCoMn–NC stabilizes OOH* more strongly than Mn–NC while avoiding the overly strong OH* binding at the multimetal sites of FeCo–NC, thereby facilitating the initial intermediate formation and final hydroxyl removal. Its ΔG_(OH*) of 0.85 eV lies close to the volcano optimum, corresponding to moderate intermediate binding. These results show that activity analysis of catalysts in which particles and dispersed metal sites coexist should start from the surface coverage and coordination formed under operating conditions and connect specific bonding interactions with the reaction steps.

METHODS

Computational and Experimental Methods. Full experimental and computational procedures are provided in Sections 1–2 of the Supporting Information. These include catalyst synthesis, structural characterization, electrochemical measurements, operando ATR-SEIRAS, structural modeling, surface coverage thermodynamics, ORR free-energy calculations, and electronic-structure analysis. The coverage candidates and the total energies used for the ORR pathways are provided in Tables S10 and S11, respectively. XAS spectra were processed with Larch and analyzed by Morlet wavelet transforms.^(25,26) Calculations used the spin-polarized Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with projector augmented-wave (PAW) potentials, the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional, and D3(BJ) dispersion,^(27–32) together with Bader and COHP analyses.^(33–38) Coverage formation free energies and ORR free energies were evaluated with the computational hydrogen electrode method, with each model referenced to its own clean structure.^(15,16)
