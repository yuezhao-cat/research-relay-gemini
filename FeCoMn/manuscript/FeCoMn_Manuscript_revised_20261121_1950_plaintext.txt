Abstract

Metal particles often coexist with single atoms in pyrolyzed
metal–nitrogen–carbon (M–N–C) catalysts, yet models usually treat bare
single atoms as the only active sites. How particles and surface
coverage under working conditions affect intermediate binding at
adjacent single atoms remains unclear. Here, we prepare an FeCoMn–NC
catalyst combining FeCo alloy particles and Mn single atoms. In 0.1 M
KOH, FeCoMn–NC achieves an oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) half-wave
potential of 0.885 V versus RHE, outperforming Pt/C, FeCo–NC, and Mn–NC.
FeCoMn–NC also retains 96.5% of its initial current after 238 h.
Operando infrared spectroscopy shows that surface species change with
potential, so we calculated surface coverage under working conditions.
At 0.78 V versus RHE, O*-covered FeCo alloy particles keep an axial OH
on adjacent Mn single atoms, whereas Mn without particles forms Mn=O.
Compared with Mn=O, the much weaker axial Mn–OH bond lets Mn bind OOH*
firmly enough for OOH* formation. Without the axial OH, Mn binds ORR
intermediates too strongly. The axial OH pulls Mn out of the N₄ plane,
and opposite-side intermediate adsorption draws Mn back. Drawing Mn back
consumes part of the binding energy and weakens excessive adsorption. At
0.78 V versus RHE, OH* removal turns downhill and OOH* formation stays
nearly thermoneutral, placing FeCoMn–NC near the volcano apex. FeCo
alloy particles thus determine the axial ligand of adjacent Mn single
atoms, keeping intermediate binding neither too strong nor too weak.

INTRODUCTION

Metal–nitrogen–carbon (M–N–C) catalysts are promising alternatives to
platinum for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) in fuel cells and
metal–air batteries.^(1,2,12) Their ORR activity depends on how strongly
the active sites bind oxygen-containing intermediates.^(5,12)
Excessively strong binding impedes the removal of the intermediates,
whereas weak binding makes their formation unfavorable.^(5,6) Improving
M–N–C catalysts therefore requires effective ways to tune intermediate
binding.

Researchers have tuned the binding of ORR intermediates by modifying the
coordinating atoms and carbon framework of M–N–C catalysts or by
introducing neighboring metal species.[D] In pyrolyzed M–N–C catalysts,
metal atoms readily aggregate into particles, especially at high metal
contents, and particles encapsulated in carbon remain after acid
leaching, so metal particles commonly coexist with single metal
atoms.[F] The activity of such catalysts is usually attributed to the
single atoms alone.[G] A few studies have examined the particles and
shown that metal particles or clusters near single metal atoms can
produce highly active catalysts.^(7,8) For example, Fe clusters change
the distribution of electronic charge around neighboring Fe–N sites and
thereby adjust how strongly these sites bind the reaction
intermediates.⁸ These analyses were based on the initial structures of
the catalysts. Under reaction conditions, however, both the metal
particles and the single atoms are covered by species from the
electrolyte.[E] How the metal particles affect the binding of ORR
intermediates at the neighboring single atoms under this coverage
remains to be clarified.

Here, we report an FeCoMn–NC catalyst in which FeCo alloy particles
coexist with Mn single atoms. In 0.1 M KOH, FeCoMn–NC reaches a
half-wave potential of 0.885 V versus RHE, outperforming commercial
Pt/C, FeCo–NC, and Mn–NC. Operando infrared spectroscopy detects OOH*,
an intermediate that exists only in the associative ORR pathway, and
shows that the species at the catalyst surface change with potential. We
therefore calculated the surface coverage of the three catalysts at
different potentials before the ORR takes place. At a representative ORR
potential of 0.78 V versus RHE, the FeCo particles are covered by O*,
and the adjacent Mn single atoms are coordinated by an axial OH. In
particle-free Mn–NC, the Mn single atoms are coordinated by an axial O
instead. On these covered surfaces, the ORR intermediates bind to the Mn
single atoms in FeCoMn–NC and Mn–NC. In Mn–NC, the strong axial Mn=O
bond leaves Mn binding the OOH* intermediate too weakly, which hinders
OOH* formation. In FeCoMn–NC, the much weaker axial Mn–OH bond allows Mn
to bind OOH* more strongly, which facilitates OOH* formation. In
FeCo–NC, by contrast, the FeCo particles bind the O* intermediate too
strongly, which hinders the conversion of O* to OH*. FeCoMn–NC therefore
binds the ORR intermediates moderately and lies near the apex of the
activity volcano, which explains why FeCoMn–NC shows the highest
measured ORR activity. These results show that FeCo particles cause the
adjacent Mn single atoms to be coordinated by an axial OH, and this
axial OH gives these Mn single atoms moderate binding of the ORR
intermediates.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

FeCoMn–NC was prepared by pyrolysis of an Fe/Co/Mn-containing zeolitic
imidazolate framework (ZIF) precursor (Figure 1a). The precursor was
synthesized from the metal sources and 2-methylimidazole in methanol and
then pyrolyzed at 1000 °C for 2 h under Ar/H₂. The product was not
acid-leached, so that the metal particles exposed at the surface of the
carbon framework were retained. FeCo–NC and Mn–NC were prepared by the
same procedure without the Mn source and without the Fe and Co sources,
respectively.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Figure 1b) shows that the carbon
framework of FeCoMn–NC retains the rhombic dodecahedral shape of the ZIF
precursor and is approximately 1 μm across. Transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) images show metal particles, predominantly 20–100 nm in
size, dispersed throughout the carbon matrix (Figure 1c and Figure S1).
These particles are primarily body-centered cubic (bcc) FeCo. Their
X-ray diffraction (XRD) reflections at 44.77°, 65.23°, and 82.69°
correspond to the bcc FeCo (110), (200), and (211) planes, respectively
(Figure 1d). The reflection near 25.9° corresponds to the carbon (002)
planes. Two weak peaks near 43.9° and 51.1°, indexed to the (111) and
(200) planes of a face-centered cubic (fcc) structure, indicate a minor
fcc phase. In the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image, a particle is
surrounded by graphitic carbon layers with a lattice spacing of
approximately 0.34 nm, which corresponds to the carbon (002) planes
(Figure 1e and Figure S2). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)
mapping locates Fe and Co in the particles and Mn throughout the carbon
matrix (Figure 1f). These results suggest that FeCoMn–NC consists of
FeCo alloy particles dispersed in the carbon matrix and Mn species
homogeneously distributed throughout the matrix.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) probes the surface composition
and chemical states of FeCoMn–NC (Figure S3 and Table S1). The N 1s
spectrum is deconvoluted into pyridinic, metal-coordinated, pyrrolic,
graphitic, and oxidized N components, with graphitic N as the largest
fraction (41.6%). The metal-coordinated N component at 399.3 eV supports
the presence of metal–N sites. The Co 2p spectrum contains both metallic
and oxidized components, whereas the Mn 2p spectrum contains only
oxidized components. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) provides
element-specific information on the local structures of Fe, Co, and Mn.
The Fe and Co K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES)
spectra resemble those of the corresponding metal foils more closely
than those of the oxide references, indicating that Fe and Co are
predominantly metallic (Figure 2a, b). In contrast, the Mn K-edge XANES
spectrum resembles that of MnPc more closely than that of Mn foil,
indicating that Mn is not metallic (Figure 2c).

The Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS)
spectra of Fe and Co exhibit strong peaks near 2.2 Å, assigned to
metal–metal scattering (Figure 2d, e). The Mn spectrum instead shows a
main peak near 1.3 Å without a resolved metal–metal scattering peak,
consistent with predominantly light-element neighbors (Figure 2f).
Fitting with a two-shell bcc structural model yields coordination-shell
distances of 2.46 and 2.84 Å for Fe and 2.45 and 2.84 Å for Co. These
values agree with the first- and second-shell distances of bcc FeCo
(2.47 and 2.86 Å for a = 2.857 Å) and thus support the XRD assignment.
First-shell fitting of the Mn spectrum yields an average Mn–N/O
coordination number of 4.9 ± 0.6 and a bond distance of 1.99 ± 0.04 Å
(Figures S4 and S5 and Table S2). The wavelet-transform intensity maxima
of Fe and Co occur at relatively high k, further supporting metal–metal
coordination (Figure 2g, h). The Mn intensity is instead concentrated at
low k, consistent with light-element coordination (Figure 2i and Figure
S6). Together, the XRD, EDS, XPS, and XAS results show that FeCoMn–NC
contains bcc FeCo alloy particles and Mn single atoms.

The ORR activity was evaluated by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) on a
rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) in O₂-saturated 0.1 M KOH. At 1600
rpm, FeCoMn–NC exhibits a half-wave potential of 0.885 V versus RHE
(Figure 3a, b and Table S3). This value exceeds those of FeCo–NC and
commercial Pt/C by 21 mV and that of Mn–NC by 45 mV. The Tafel slope of
FeCoMn–NC (40 mV dec⁻¹) is also the lowest of the four catalysts,
indicating faster apparent ORR kinetics (Figure 3c). At 0.90 V, the
kinetic current density of FeCoMn–NC (2.89 mA cm⁻²) is much higher than
those of the other three catalysts (Figure 3d). The kinetic current is
corrected for mass transport and therefore reflects the intrinsic
activity. FeCoMn–NC outperforms both FeCo–NC and Mn–NC in all three
metrics. Its activity therefore cannot be attributed to either component
alone.

Figure 3e and Table S4 compare the half-wave potential of FeCoMn–NC with
those of reported M–N–C catalysts in 0.1 M KOH.^(8,17–19) FeCoMn–NC
shows the highest value in this comparison, exceeding those of
single-atom, single-metal, and bimetallic catalysts. The limiting
current of all four catalysts increases with the rotation rate from 400
to 2500 rpm, as expected for diffusion-limited O₂ reduction (Figure S7).
Cyclic voltammetry confirms that the reduction near 0.8 V originates
from O₂ rather than from the catalyst itself. A cathodic peak appears at
this potential in O₂-saturated electrolyte but is absent in N₂-saturated
electrolyte (Figure S8). FeCoMn–NC also shows a larger capacitive
current than FeCo–NC at the same scan rates, indicating a larger
electrochemically accessible surface area. The RRDE measurements provide
the electron-transfer number (n) and the H₂O₂ yield (Figure 3f).
FeCoMn–NC shows an average n of 3.83 and an average H₂O₂ yield of 8.3%
over 0.30–0.75 V, consistent with a predominantly four-electron pathway.
This selectivity is slightly lower than that of Pt/C (n = 3.94, 3.1%
H₂O₂), and the difference arises mainly at low potentials. The H₂O₂
yield of FeCoMn–NC decreases as the potential increases, and above about
0.75 V it falls below that of Pt/C. The measured ring currents give the
same comparison (Figure S9). FeCoMn–NC retains 96.5% of its initial
current after 238 h at 0.516 V and 1600 rpm (Figure 3g). To examine
whether the half-wave potential shifts, LSV curves were recorded before
and after a 31.6 h test. The half-wave potential shows no decrease (+0.8
mV, Figure 3h and Figure S10). Both results show that FeCoMn–NC is
stable under prolonged operation.

Operando attenuated total reflection surface-enhanced infrared
absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) was used to monitor the interfacial
species on FeCoMn–NC in O₂-saturated 0.1 M KOH (Figure 4). Figure 4a
shows the difference spectra between 3650 and 1000 cm⁻¹ at potentials
from 1.2 to 0 V versus RHE, referenced to the spectrum at 1.2 V. The
bands near 3307 and 1633 cm⁻¹ correspond to the O–H stretching and H–O–H
bending modes of interfacial water, respectively, and the band near 1215
cm⁻¹ is assigned to the ORR intermediate OOH*.^(20,21) Figure 4b shows
these three regions enlarged, with the spectra offset by potential. As
the potential is lowered from 1.2 to 0 V, the negative O–H and H–O–H
bands deepen, showing that the state of the interfacial water changes
with potential. Meanwhile, the positive OOH* band grows, indicating that
the ORR on FeCoMn–NC follows the associative pathway (Figure 4c).
Overall, the FeCoMn–NC surface remains covered by interfacial species
whose composition changes with potential.

The free energies of many different coverage configurations were
therefore compared to determine the surface states of the three
catalysts at various potentials and pH (Figure 5 and Table S5). As shown
in Figure 5a–c, the surfaces of all three catalysts are covered by H* at
negative potentials, and adsorbed O-containing species become more
stable as the potential increases. As shown in Figure 5d–f, the surface
coverage of FeCoMn–NC changes from 8H* to 8O* with increasing potential,
then to 8O* + Mn–OH*, and finally to 8O* + Mn–O*. The surface coverage
of FeCo–NC changes only from 8H* to 8O*, whereas that of Mn–NC changes
from 3H* to the clean surface and then to 1O* and 2O*. Figures S13–S16
and S17–S22 give the corresponding free-energy data and structures,
respectively. At 0.78 V versus RHE (a representative ORR potential)¹¹
and pH 13, which corresponds to the 0.1 M KOH electrolyte, the surface
coverage is 8O* + Mn–OH* for FeCoMn–NC, 8O* for FeCo–NC, and 1O* for
Mn–NC. The corresponding structures are shown in Figure 5g and Figure
S24. To identify the role of the FeCo particles, we removed the particle
from the FeCoMn–NC model and kept all other atoms and calculation
settings unchanged. Without the particle, the axial ligand on Mn changes
from OH to O at 0.48 V, whereas with the particle this change occurs
only at 0.96 V (Table S7). The FeCo particles therefore keep the
adjacent Mn single atoms in the Mn–OH state at the representative ORR
potential of 0.78 V, whereas without the particles Mn is oxidized to
Mn=O. The FeCo particles thus determine the surface coverage of
FeCoMn–NC under reaction conditions, namely O* on the particles and an
axial OH on the adjacent Mn single atoms.

On these covered surfaces, obtained with the actual potential and
solution pH taken into account, the free energies of the four-step
associative pathway of O₂ → OOH* → O* → OH* → H₂O were then calculated.
Here, ΔG₁, ΔG₂, ΔG₃, and ΔG₄ refer to the reaction free energies of OOH*
formation, OOH* conversion to O*, O* conversion to OH*, and OH* removal,
respectively (Table S8). On FeCoMn–NC and Mn–NC, the intermediates bind
to Mn, and on FeCo–NC to the O-covered particle (Table S9 and Figure
S25). Figure 5h compares the three pathways at 0.78 V, and Figure S26
shows them at 0 V. On FeCo–NC, O* binds too strongly, so its conversion
to OH* is uphill by 0.44 eV. On Mn–NC, OOH* binds too weakly, so its
formation is uphill by 0.52 eV. On FeCoMn–NC, the largest step is OOH*
formation, which is uphill by less than 0.01 eV, and the other three
steps are downhill. The limiting potentials of FeCoMn–NC, FeCo–NC, and
Mn–NC are therefore 0.78, 0.34, and 0.26 V, corresponding to theoretical
overpotentials of 0.45, 0.89, and 0.97 V (Table S8). The order of the
limiting potentials agrees with that of the measured half-wave
potentials (Figure 3b), and the moderate intermediate binding of
FeCoMn–NC accounts for its higher activity than FeCo–NC and Mn–NC.

To understand the origin of this moderate binding, we analyzed the
electronic structure of the reaction sites before and after the
intermediates adsorb, together with the bonding and charge transfer
between the sites and the intermediates (Figure 6). Before the reaction,
the Mn atoms in FeCoMn–NC and Mn–NC have nearly the same occupied d-band
center, and both are lower than that of the bonded metals in FeCo–NC
(Figure 6a and Table S10). The d-band center therefore cannot explain
why the two Mn atoms bind the intermediates differently. In contrast,
the axial Mn–OH bond in FeCoMn–NC is much weaker than the axial Mn=O
bond in Mn–NC, with negative integrated crystal orbital Hamilton
population (−ICOHP) values of 2.64 and 8.03 eV (Figure 6b). The Mn atom
in FeCoMn–NC also has a much larger local magnetic moment (Figure 6c).
Its spin-up and spin-down 3d states are strongly unbalanced, whereas
those of Mn in Mn–NC are nearly balanced, with almost all 3d electrons
paired. The difference between the Mn atoms in FeCoMn–NC and Mn–NC
therefore lies in the axial ligand adsorbed from the electrolyte before
the reaction, and this ligand is set by the FeCo particles (Table S7).

After the intermediates adsorb, the metal 3d states overlap with the O
2p states of the intermediates below the Fermi level in each catalyst,
forming metal–O bonds (Figure 6d, Figures S27 and S28). The Mn–O bonds
formed with OOH* and OH* are stronger in FeCoMn–NC than in Mn–NC, as
shown by their larger −ICOHP values (Figure 6e, Figure S29 and Table
S10). Mn in FeCoMn–NC therefore holds OOH* more firmly, which makes OOH*
formation easier than on Mn–NC, where this step limits the ORR. At the
same time, the axial OH prevents Mn from binding the ORR intermediates
too strongly. Without the axial OH, Mn shifts 0.46 Å out of the N₄ plane
toward adsorbed OH*. OH* therefore binds so strongly that its removal
limits the ORR at 0.52 V (Table S11). By contrast, the axial OH pulls Mn
0.44 Å out of the N₄ plane toward the axial OH. An ORR intermediate
adsorbing on the opposite side then draws Mn back toward the N₄ plane
(Figure S30a). Drawing Mn back consumes part of the binding energy.
Relative to Mn without the axial OH, OH* adsorption weakens by 0.34 eV,
of which 0.24 eV arises from the structural deformation and 0.10 eV from
the weaker electronic interaction between Mn and OH* (Figure S30b and
Table S11). Upon adsorption, electrons move from the catalyst to the
intermediates, and the amount transferred reflects how strongly they
interact. Both the electrons lost by the bonded metals (Figure 6f) and
those gained by the intermediates (Figure 6g and Figure S31) increase
from Mn–NC to FeCoMn–NC to FeCo–NC. FeCoMn–NC thus binds the
intermediates moderately. This moderate binding places FeCoMn–NC near
the apex of the activity volcano (Figure 6h), where OOH* formation and
OH* removal are relatively balanced. Because ΔG_(OOH*) − ΔG_(OH*) is
nearly constant for the three catalysts (Table S8), the limiting
potential can be approximated as a function of ΔG_(OH*) alone,^(5,22,23)
which gives the volcano in Figure 6h.^(6,24) The construction of this
volcano is described in Section 2.8 of the Supporting Information.
Figure 6i summarizes the mechanism. At the ORR working potential and pH
13, O* and OH from the electrolyte adsorb on the FeCo particles and on
the neighboring Mn, respectively, with the OH bound axially. The FeCo
particles keep this axial OH on Mn, and the axial OH pulls Mn out of the
N₄ plane, so that Mn binds the intermediates neither too strongly nor
too weakly. The ORR proceeds on this Mn single atom.

CONCLUSIONS

An FeCoMn–NC catalyst with FeCo alloy particles and Mn single atoms
reaches an ORR half-wave potential of 0.885 V versus RHE, surpassing
Pt/C, FeCo–NC, and Mn–NC. At pH 13, O*-covered FeCo alloy particles
shift the Mn–OH to Mn=O transition from 0.48 to 0.96 V versus RHE.
Adjacent Mn single atoms thus keep an axial OH at 0.78 V versus RHE,
whereas Mn–NC forms Mn=O. Compared with Mn=O, the weaker axial Mn–OH
bond lets Mn bind OOH* more firmly. Meanwhile, the axial OH pulls Mn
0.44 Å out of the N₄ plane, and opposite-side adsorption draws Mn back.
Relative to overly strong binding without the axial OH, OH* adsorption
weakens by 0.34 eV (0.24 eV from structural deformation and 0.10 eV from
weaker electronic interaction). FeCoMn–NC thus achieves a limiting
potential of 0.78 V versus RHE near the volcano apex, exceeding FeCo–NC
(0.34 V) and Mn–NC (0.26 V). Coexisting particle and single-atom
catalysts should be analyzed on covered surfaces under reaction
conditions, where particles determine the axial ligand that balances
intermediate binding through electronic structure and geometry.

METHODS

Computational and Experimental Methods. Full experimental and
computational procedures are provided in Sections 1–2 of the Supporting
Information. These include catalyst synthesis, structural
characterization, electrochemical measurements, operando ATR-SEIRAS,
structural modeling, surface coverage thermodynamics, ORR free-energy
calculations, and electronic-structure analysis. The coverage candidates
and the total energies used for the ORR pathways are provided in Tables
S12 and S13, respectively. XAS spectra were processed with Larch and
analyzed by Morlet wavelet transforms.^(25,26) Calculations used the
spin-polarized Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with projector
augmented-wave (PAW) potentials, the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)
functional, and D3(BJ) dispersion,^(27–32) together with Bader and COHP
analyses.^(33–38) Coverage formation free energies and ORR free energies
were evaluated with the computational hydrogen electrode method, with
each model referenced to its own clean structure.^(15,16)
